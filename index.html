How it works

Early computers stored data on cassette as sound. Here, a zero is four cycles at 1,200 Hz. A one is eight cycles at 2,400 Hz. Both last 1/300 second.

Each character travels as a start bit, eight data bits (least significant first), and two stop bits. Click a byte to see those eleven windows on the scope.

The receiver measures the strength of both pitches in each window. A weak or ambiguous tone, or invalid framing, produces an unreadable byte, shown as □. A confidently decoded but different byte counts as a mismatch. The original message is used only for the recovery score.

Try Damaged tape. The gap removes part of the signal near its center. Click an unreadable byte to inspect it, then move Dropout back to zero. Noise mixes a repeatable hiss into the samples.

This classroom receiver uses a known bit clock and frame alignment. The WAV saves the full recording as 16-bit audio, including any damage and the leader and trailer tones.
